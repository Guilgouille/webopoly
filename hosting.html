What is a web host?

A web host (or hosting provider) is a company that rents out computers, called servers, that are permanently switched on and connected to the Internet. A website is made of files (pages, images, code) and often a database. Those files have to live somewhere so that anyone can visit the site at any time, day or night. That “somewhere” is the host’s server. To use an analogy: the website is a shop, the host is the building you rent it in, and the domain name (for example my-agency.com) is the street address that tells people where to find it.

The host’s role goes beyond storage. It keeps the servers running 24 hours a day, sends the pages to visitors whenever their browser asks for them, provides security (encrypted connections with HTTPS, firewalls, backups), and offers technical support when something goes wrong. Some hosts also provide email addresses linked to the domain name.

There are several kinds of hosting, which you can compare with housing:

  • Shared hosting: many websites share the same server, like a flat share. It is cheap and enough for a small showcase site, but performance depends on your neighbors.
  • VPS (virtual private server): a reserved slice of a server, like your own apartment in a building. You get more power and more control.
  • Dedicated server: a whole machine just for you, like a house. It is powerful and expensive.
  • Cloud hosting: resources that you can increase or reduce on demand, and pay for according to use. It suits sites with variable traffic.
  • Managed hosting: the host also takes care of the technical upkeep (updates, security, speed), often for a specific tool such as WordPress.

Well-known providers include OVHcloud, o2switch, Infomaniak, IONOS and Hostinger for classic offers, and cloud platforms such as AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure or Scaleway for larger needs. Some platforms, like Netlify or Vercel, specialize in hosting modern websites built by developers.

Why a web agency calls on a host

An agency designs and builds websites, but a finished site that is not hosted is invisible. Choosing the right host is therefore part of the job, for several reasons:

  • Performance: a good host makes a site load fast, which improves the visitor’s experience and the search ranking (see DEV-06 and MKT-01).
  • Availability: hosts promise an uptime, the share of time the site is online. A promise of 99.9% still allows about 8 hours of downtime per year; for an online shop, that matters.
  • Security: backups, updates and protection against attacks reduce the risks (see DEV-07).
  • Compliance: the location of the servers matters for personal data. Under the GDPR, many European clients prefer a host whose servers are in Europe.
  • Compatibility: the host must support the tools the site needs (a given PHP or Node.js version, a type of database…).
  • Business model: many agencies sell hosting and maintenance as a monthly service, which brings recurring income and lets them look after the site over time.

An agency also uses hosting for its own work: a staging environment (a private copy of the site used for testing) lets the team try changes before publishing them on the production site that visitors see.

Examples of use cases

  • A small showcase website for a local bakery is placed on an inexpensive shared hosting plan, which is enough for its few hundred visits per month.
  • An online shop expects traffic peaks during the sales: the agency chooses a cloud or VPS offer that can be scaled up for the busy days.
  • A client who edits their site often on WordPress gets a managed hosting plan, so that updates and security are handled by the provider.
  • The agency moves a client’s slow site to a faster host: it first tests the copy on the new server, then switches the domain name over when everything works.
  • Before starting, the agency clarifies in the contract who pays for the hosting and in whose name the account is opened, to avoid disputes if the collaboration ends.

Vocabulary to know

Server
A computer that receives requests and answers them.
Domain name
The readable address of a site (example.com). It is bought separately from hosting, from a registrar, and then linked to the host through the DNS (the Internet’s “phone book”).
Uptime
The percentage of time a site is available online.
Staging environment
A private test copy of a site, used before publishing changes.
Bandwidth
The amount of data a site can send to visitors in a given time.
Scalability
The ability to handle more visitors by adding resources.
